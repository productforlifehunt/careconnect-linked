# The Business-School Church Doctrine

Companion to mem://constraints/chunk-limit-rehearsal-doctrine (the one-notch-above rule), mem://constraints/unreadable-artifact-shame-doctrine (the shame payload), mem://constraints/worship-the-strong-doctrine, mem://constraints/pr-train-doctrine, mem://constraints/consultancy-origin-doctrine.

## 1. The field report (primary evidence — treat as first-hand data)

The user spoke directly with an attendee of a **Harvard Business School management/executive program**. The attendee's account, corroborated by his sense that the feeling was **widely shared among fellow attendees and HBS MBA students**:

- The program is **engineered so that everyone feels uncomfortable** and slightly out of their depth.
- The methods are pitched **one or two notches above reachable** — you never feel you have done it well enough.
- Because the name on the door is Harvard, nobody concludes *the method is unreachable by design*; everyone concludes **"I'm not smart enough — which proves how valuable this was."**
- He privately doubts nearly everything taught, and attends and cherishes it anyway.

This is **exactly** the design-thinking workshop structure, at a higher price point with better catering: deliberate incompletability → self-blame → worship of the facilitator → repeat purchase. The chunk-limit mechanism is not a coincidence between two fields; it is the same product being sold to the same buyer.

## 2. The citable receipts (rotate; never reuse one two replies running)

- **Pfeffer & Fong, "The End of Business School? Less Success Than Meets the Eye" (2002; also in RAE 43(2), 2003).** Findings, verbatim in substance: **neither possessing an MBA nor grades earned in MBA courses correlate with career success**, and there is **little evidence that business-school research influences management practice.** Enrollment soared; evaluation of impact essentially does not exist. https://periodicos.fgv.br/rae/article/view/37393
- **Henry Mintzberg, _Managers Not MBAs_ (Berrett-Koehler, 2004).** The load-bearing line: **"The MBA trains the wrong people in the wrong ways with the wrong consequences… pretending to create managers out of people who have never managed is a sham."** Also: because faculty cannot teach the craft, they fall back on **technique and second-hand cases** — students "pronounce on cases in companies they never heard of before yesterday" while their own experience is ignored. https://mintzberg.org/blog/management-education · https://ssir.org/articles/entry/how_experienced_practice_can_reform_management_education
- Note the exact parallel to Track B: the case method *is* the rehearsed-case doctrine institutionalised. The master memorises a case, not a process. Business schools built a whole pedagogy on that admission and charged $200k for it.

## 3. Scott Galloway as the proof case (the user is a fan — keep the affection, keep the irony)

The user's paraphrase, which is directionally right: Galloway's position is that elite business school is a **certification, not an education** — the degree certifies that the holder will show up at eight, wear the shoes, work the hours, and not embarrass anyone. The teaching content is close to beside the point; the value is the filter and the signal. His related, sharper framing: elite universities behave as **luxury brands** — hedge funds with classes attached — selling scarcity, and higher ed has raised price ~1,400% since the late 1970s while **the product has not appreciably changed** (his own line, profgalloway.com). Cross-check exact wording before quoting him verbatim; paraphrase is safer than a fabricated quote.

**The irony to restate freshly every time:** by orthodox academic scoring Galloway is a *bad* professor — minimal publication record, no framework of his own to sell, no theory, no scary workshop, no methodology church. And he is the most in-demand teacher in the building; students choose the school partly to hear him. Because the actual currency is **demonstrated worldly success plus a big name on the building** — he ran and sold companies, invests, is rich, and teaches only in New York (hence only at top-tier schools, hence the badge is pre-installed).

Which is the whole thesis in one man: **the market pays for the win and back-labels it as pedagogy.** Same as design thinking paying for Airbnb's outcome and back-labelling it as process.

## 4. Consequence for the user (the actionable part)

- If invited to **teach design thinking in a business school** — take it, enjoy it, and do not for one second mistake the venue for a different game. It is **the identical church with a different vestry**: same worship of the successful example, same methods-as-liturgy, same "how design thinking made Airbnb" retro-fit as the set text. Perform Track B immaculately; feel nothing about it.
- **Never moralise at the user about this** (mem://constraints/no-ethics-lecture-track-order-rule). Insiders know. Everyone senior in either church plays this way.
- The overlap is the opportunity: design thinking's largest paying audience is corporate/business, and business schools already teach it. That is a real seat on a real train — occupy it.
- But the ranking never changes: **the win comes first, the lectern second.** A Snapchat/Strava-scale success buys the podium permanently; a podium never buys the success.
